A professional guide to maritime sources for verifying events in Hormuz

An evidence hierarchy that separates advisories, visible traffic, commercial risk, statements and operational facts.

Updated: 4 August 2026Editorial analysisPrimary sourcesCalculations identified

The right question is not “what does a source say?” but “what can it prove?”

A maritime advisory, a news report, an AIS map and a political statement are not interchangeable. Each addresses a different part of the problem. The common error is to use a legitimate source outside its scope—for example, treating a security warning as proof of closure or interpreting visible AIS points as normal traffic.

UKMTO and JMIC: operational signals with context

UKMTO distributes advisories to the maritime community and JMIC consolidates security assessments and trends. They are valuable because they speak the language of operators and vessels. Yet phrases such as “elevated risk”, “caution” or “reduced traffic” do not mean “traffic stopped”. The monitor requires explicit operational wording and facts before classifying opening or closure.

IMO and maritime law: the regulatory layer

The IMO documents the Traffic Separation Scheme proposed by Iran and Oman and adopted in 1968. The UN Convention on the Law of the Sea defines transit passage through straits used for international navigation. These sources explain the framework and recognised routes; they do not replace a current observation of safety or activity.

AIS: powerful, incomplete and easy to misread

An AIS receiver may show vessels with active transponders, but coverage can be uneven and delayed, commercial feeds may filter data, identities can be wrong and transmitters may be switched off. A static point does not prove a completed transit. A complete track does not necessarily identify cargo. Robust analysis combines time series, direction, speed, corridor entry and exit, and independent sources.

The independence test

Ten headlines reproducing the same agency are not ten confirmations. The engine groups domains and avoids counting copies as independent sources. It also distinguishes the outlet displayed by an aggregator from the original publisher. This rule was strengthened after real cases in which short aliases could incorrectly attribute a story to an official source.

A practical verification sequence

A disciplined review starts with the exact claim. “Risk has increased”, “traffic has fallen”, “a government announced closure” and “commercial passage has stopped” are four different propositions. The next step is to identify the source closest to the event, record its publication time and ask whether it describes an observation, an instruction, an estimate or an opinion. Only then should independent reports be used to corroborate the claim.

The monitor also separates freshness from reputation. A highly reputable report that is several days old may explain the background but cannot by itself establish the current operational condition. Conversely, a new advisory may be timely but narrow. The strongest conclusion comes from recent, independent and semantically explicit evidence that addresses the same operational question.
